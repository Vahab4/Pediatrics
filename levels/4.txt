CONGENITAL AND PERINATAL INFECTIONS
1. GENERAL CLINICAL PRESENTATION & DIAGNOSTIC WORKUP
Shared Clinical Features of Congenital Infections (TORCH):
• Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR)
• CNS abnormalities: microcephaly, hydrocephalus, and intracranial calcifications
• Hematologic and cutaneous signs: thrombocytopenia and "blueberry muffin" skin rash (extramedullary dermal hematopoiesis)
• Hepatobiliary involvement: hepatosplenomegaly and conjugated hyperbilirubinemia
• Ocular findings: chorioretinitis and cataracts
Etiologies (TORCH & Others):
• T: Toxoplasmosis
• O (Other): Syphilis, HIV, Lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV), Parvovirus B19, Varicella
• R: Rubella
• C: Cytomegalovirus (CMV)
• H: Herpes simplex virus (HSV)
Diagnostic Evaluation:
• General Tests: Complete blood count (CBC), total and direct bilirubin, liver enzymes, total IgM, long bone radiographs, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, ophthalmologic exam, and CNS imaging.
• Pathogen-Specific Tests:
	• Toxoplasmosis: IgM and IgG serology
	• Syphilis: Quantitative RPR; CSF routine studies and quantitative VDRL
	• Rubella: Rubella-specific IgM; viral culture from eye, urine, and nasopharynx
	• CMV: Urine culture for CMV (note: viral shedding may be intermittent), IgM/IgG titers
	• HSV: IgM/IgG serology, CSF HSV PCR, viral cultures
	• Parvovirus B19: PCR
	• LCMV: Infant IgM and IgG, maternal IgG, viral culture
2. SPECIFIC CONGENITAL INFECTIONS
Toxoplasmosis:
• Etiology & Transmission: Caused by the protozoan Toxoplasma gondii (cats are the definitive host). Transmitted via ingestion of oocysts (cat litter, unwashed produce, contaminated soil/water), tissue cysts in raw/undercooked meat, organ/blood donation, or transplacentally via tachyzoites.
• Clinical Presentation: 70–90% are asymptomatic at birth. Symptomatic infants present with maculopapular rash, thrombocytopenia, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, hydrocephalus, microcephaly, chorioretinitis, seizures, and deafness.
• Diagnosis & Treatment: IgM/IgG titers and diffuse intracranial calcifications on imaging. Treated with pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine.
Congenital Syphilis:
• Clinical Presentation: 50% are asymptomatic at birth. Early signs include fever, lymphadenopathy, irritability, failure to thrive, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, meningitis, and "snuffles" (copious serous/hemorrhagic rhinitis).
• Mucocutaneous & Hematologic Signs: Palmar/plantar bullae (pemphigus syphiliticus), maculopapular rash on trunk and limbs, mucosal lesions, condylomata lata, anemia (bone marrow arrest or hemolysis), thrombocytopenia, and leukopenia or leukocytosis.
• Skeletal Changes: Osteochondritis of long bones (humerus, tibia).
• Diagnosis: Quantitative RPR, CSF exam (cell count, protein, VDRL), CBC, liver enzymes, long bone X-rays, direct demonstration of spirochetes in tissue/fluid, and HIV testing.
• Treatment & Follow-up:
	• Aqueous crystalline Penicillin G: 100,000–150,000 units/kg/day for 10–14 days (administered as 50,000 units/kg/dose IV every 12 hours for the first 7 days, then every 8 hours for a total of 10 days).
	• Alternative: Procaine penicillin G 50,000 units/kg/day IM once daily for 10 days (note: may not achieve adequate CSF concentrations for neurosyphilis; ampicillin is not an acceptable alternative).
	• Follow-up: Repeat RPR titers at 3, 6, and 12 months.
Congenital Rubella Syndrome:
• Transmission & Prevention: Vertical transplacental passage; highest risk of severe malformations occurs during early gestation. Non-immune pregnant women must avoid exposure and should not be vaccinated during pregnancy (though no malformations have been documented from inadvertent vaccination); administer vaccine postpartum.
• Clinical Findings: 50% are asymptomatic at birth. Classic triad includes sensorineural hearing loss, congenital heart defects (patent ductus arteriosus [PDA], peripheral pulmonic stenosis), and ocular defects (cataracts, chorioretinitis, glaucoma). Also causes intellectual disability, microcephaly, blueberry muffin rash, and metaphyseal radiolucencies ("celery stalk" bone appearance).
• Diagnosis & Treatment: Rubella-specific IgM and viral culture (nasopharynx, throat, blood, urine, CSF). Management is supportive.
Cytomegalovirus (CMV):
• Epidemiology & Transmission: Transplacental or perinatal acquisition from primary or reactivated maternal CMV. Incidence is ~2.5% of live births, with 95% being asymptomatic at birth.
• Clinical Findings (Symptomatic): Microcephaly, periventricular intracranial calcifications, thrombocytopenia, petechiae/purpura, conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, elevated liver enzymes/liver failure, interstitial pneumonitis, sensorineural hearing loss, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, chorioretinitis, and intestinal pseudo-obstruction.
• Diagnosis & Treatment: Acute/convalescent IgM and IgG titers, urine CMV culture, CNS imaging, and eye exam. Treatment includes supportive care (e.g., platelet transfusions), ganciclovir (may reduce neurodevelopmental sequelae, though efficacy is limited), and CMV hyperimmune globulin.
Lymphocytic Choriomeningitis Virus (LCMV):
• Etiology & Presentation: Rodent-borne arenavirus. Adults experience a flu-like illness (fever, malaise, myalgia, retro-orbital headache, photophobia). Congenital infection causes severe CNS and ocular damage: hydrocephalus, chorioretinitis, intracranial calcifications, microcephaly, intellectual disability, and vision loss.
• Diagnosis & Treatment: Viral culture and acute/convalescent antibody titers. Treatment is supportive.
Parvovirus B19:
• Clinical Associations: Erythema infectiosum (fifth disease), transient aplastic crisis (in hemolytic anemias like sickle cell disease), chronic anemia in immunocompromised hosts, and acute arthritis.
• Fetal Impact: Infects erythroid precursors, causing severe fetal anemia, non-immune hydrops fetalis (pleural effusions, subcutaneous edema), and fetal demise. Managed with intrauterine blood transfusions when severe, though spontaneous resolution of hydrops can occur.
Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV):
• Congenital Varicella Syndrome (Maternal infection <20 weeks): Occurs in 1–2% of exposed fetuses; features cicatricial (zigzag) skin scarring, limb hypoplasia, and CNS/ocular defects.
• Perinatal Varicella (Maternal rash from 5 days before to 2 days after delivery): High risk of severe disseminated neonatal infection and pneumonitis due to lack of transplacental maternal IgG. Exposed infants require Varicella-Zoster Immune Globulin (VZIG) 125 units IM immediately, with IV acyclovir added if clinical signs develop.
3. PERINATALLY ACQUIRED INFECTIONS & NEONATAL HSV
Principles of Perinatal Acquisition:
• Acquired via maternal colonization or active infection in amniotic fluid, blood, genital tract secretions, breast milk, or direct skin/environmental contact. Risk depends on timing, duration of exposure, and timely prophylaxis.
• HIV Vertical Transmission: Occurs in utero, intrapartum (most common route), and postnatally via breastfeeding.
Neonatal Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV):
• Epidemiology: Acquired primarily during passage through the birth canal after rupture of membranes (ROM). Transmission risk is ~50% in primary maternal HSV vs. <1–5% in recurrent maternal HSV, and is higher in premature infants (due to lower maternal IgG transfer). Cesarean delivery reduces risk if performed within 4–6 hours of ROM. Horizontal transmission (nursery outbreaks) also occurs. Symptom onset is typically between 2 days and several weeks of life.
• Clinical Presentation: Fever, grouped skin/oral vesicles, conjunctivitis, encephalitis, seizures, respiratory distress/pneumonia, hepatitis, and a septic shock-like syndrome.
• Treatment & Exposure Management:
	• Active Disease: High-dose IV acyclovir 60 mg/kg/day divided every 8 hours for 14 days (skin/eye/mouth disease) or 21 days (CNS or disseminated disease). Add topical ophthalmic trifluridine, iododeoxyuridine, or vidarabine for ocular HSV. Mortality remains high in CNS/disseminated disease despite therapy.
	• Asymptomatic Exposure: For recurrent maternal HSV, observe closely (very low risk). For primary maternal HSV, obtain viral throat/surface cultures at 24–48 hours of life (empiric acyclovir is controversial unless symptomatic or premature).

NEONATAL JAUNDICE
1. BILIRUBIN METABOLISM & CLASSIFICATION
Bilirubin Metabolism Pathway:
1. Breakdown of red blood cells releases heme, which is converted into unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin (bound to albumin in blood).
2. In hepatocytes (transported via ligandin/Z-protein), UDP-glucuronosyltransferase conjugates indirect bilirubin with glucuronic acid to form water-soluble direct bilirubin (cholebilirubin).
3. Direct bilirubin is excreted via the biliary system into the small intestine, where bacterial proteases/flora convert it into urobilinogen.
4. Fate of Urobilinogen:
	• ~90% travels to the large intestine, oxidizes into stercobilinogen and stercobilin, and is excreted in stool (giving feces its brown color).
	• ~10% is reabsorbed into the portal vein (enterohepatic circulation) back to the liver, while a small fraction enters systemic circulation via hemorrhoidal veins and is excreted by the kidneys as urinary urobilin (giving urine its yellow color).
Classification of Neonatal Jaundice:
1. Physiological Jaundice: Appears on day 3–4 of life due to postnatal breakdown of fetal RBCs and transient hepatic enzyme immaturity. Resolves within 2 weeks in full-term infants and within 4 weeks in premature infants.
2. Suprahepatic (Hemolytic) Jaundice: Caused by accelerated RBC destruction (elevated indirect bilirubin).
3. Hepatic (Conjugation & Parenchymal) Jaundice:
	• Conjugation defects: Gilbert syndrome, Crigler-Najjar syndrome (glucuronyltransferase deficiency), congenital hypothyroidism.
	• Parenchymal liver disease: Neonatal hepatitis of various etiologies, galactosemia.
4. Subhepatic (Obstructive / Excretory) Jaundice: Dubin-Johnson syndrome, Rotor syndrome, biliary atresia, cholelithiasis.
5. HEMOLYTIC JAUNDICE & HEMOLYTIC DISEASE OF THE NEWBORN (HDN)
Etiologies of Hemolytic Jaundice:
1. Hereditary RBC membranopathies (e.g., Minkowski-Chauffard hereditary spherocytosis) and enzymopathies (e.g., G6PD deficiency).
2. Hemoglobinopathies (sickle cell disease, thalassemia).
3. Drug-induced hemolysis.
4. Large hematomas (e.g., cephalohematoma resorption).
5. Hemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN).
Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn (HDN):
• Pathogenesis: Maternal alloantibodies destroy fetal/neonatal RBCs due to blood group antigen incompatibility—most commonly ABO or Rh factor incompatibility.
• Factors Suppressing Conjugation / Worsening Hyperbilirubinemia: Hypoalbuminemia, hypoglycemia, elevated non-esterified fatty acids, hypoxia, and metabolic acidosis.
Clinical Forms & Thresholds for Visible Icterus:
• Three clinical forms of HDN: Edematous (hydrops fetalis), Icteric (jaundice), and Anemic.
• Serum Bilirubin Thresholds for Visible Skin Jaundice:
	• Adults: > 40 µmol/L (note: slides state mmol/L)
	• Full-term newborns: > 80–90 µmol/L
	• Premature infants: > 120 µmol/L
3. MANAGEMENT OF NEONATAL JAUNDICE
Treatment Modalities:
1. Phototherapy: Primary non-invasive treatment using light-blue fluorescent lamps or fiber-optic blankets to isomerize unconjugated bilirubin in the skin into water-soluble lumirubin for excretion.
2. Phenobarbital: Induces hepatic microsomal UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity to enhance bilirubin conjugation.
3. Choleretic drugs: Promote bile flow and excretion.
4. Metabolic and vitamin support: Hydration, glucose maintenance, and correction of acidosis/hypoxia.
5. Exchange (Replacement) Blood Transfusion: Indicated for severe hyperbilirubinemia to prevent kernicterus—specifically when indirect bilirubin rises at a rate ≥ 10 µmol/L per hour or exceeds a total indirect bilirubin concentration of > 340 µmol/L.
